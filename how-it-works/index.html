How It Works

Find out what actually works for you. No science degree required.

You've probably tried a new supplement, an earlier bedtime, or a cold shower and wondered: is this doing anything, or do I just want it to? ABMe is an app that answers that question properly. Here is how, in plain English.

Why "just trying it for a month" doesn't tell you much

Say you start taking magnesium and, a few weeks later, you feel like you're sleeping better. Was it the magnesium? Or was it the cooler weather, the holiday you took, the fact you stopped checking email in bed, or simply that you expected it to help?

All of those things changed in the same month. Your body doesn't label which one caused what. So when you compare "the month before" with "the month after," you're really comparing two different periods of your life, not the supplement against no supplement.

The fix is surprisingly simple: don't choose when to do the thing. Let a coin flip choose. If a coin decides which nights you take magnesium, then the weather, your stress, and your expectations get spread evenly across both kinds of nights. Any difference left over has to be the magnesium.

That's the whole idea behind ABMe. It flips the coin for you, keeps score, and tells you when there is a real difference.

What you actually do

An experiment in ABMe is a comparison between two versions of your life. Call them A and B: with the thing, and without it. You spend a stretch of time switching between the two, and the app works out which one was better for you.

01

Pick a question

Choose one of the ready-made experiments in the library, such as "Does a 10 AM caffeine cut-off deepen my sleep?" or "Do carbon-plated shoes make me faster?", or write your own. You decide what A and B mean (for example, "no caffeine after 10 AM" versus "my usual habits") and what you want to measure, like minutes of deep sleep.

02

Let the app flip the coin

Experiments are split into small chunks. Depending on the question, a chunk might be a night, a day, or a single workout. Each time a new chunk begins, you tap once and ABMe randomly tells you whether this one is an A or a B. You don't get to pick, and that is the point.

03

Live it

Do what the app told you. Skip the afternoon coffee, or don't. Wear the racing shoes, or the trainers. Then get on with your day as normal.

04

Your wearable keeps score

If you wear an Apple Watch, Oura ring, WHOOP, Garmin, Fitbit, or any other device that syncs to Apple Health, ABMe reads the result for each chunk automatically: how long you slept, your heart rate, your pace, and so on. Nothing to write down. For things no device can measure, you can type the result in yourself.

05

See the answer

ABMe sorts your results into the A pile and the B pile and compares them. Rather than a simple "yes" or "no," it shows you a range of how big the difference could be, and that range narrows as the experiment goes on. Once the range clearly sits on one side of "no difference," you have your answer. You can stop whenever the picture is clear enough for you.

Because both kinds of night are scattered across the same six weeks, they share the same weather, the same work deadlines, and the same mood swings. When you average the blue nights and the orange nights, all of that cancels out. What's left is the effect of the habit itself.

How to read the result

ABMe doesn't ask you to trust a single number. Instead it shows a range: the smallest and largest difference that is still believable given what it has seen so far. Early on, with only a handful of nights, that range is wide, because almost anything is still possible. Each new night makes it a little narrower.

The thin grey line is "no difference." By week 5 the whole range sits to its right, so the new habit is helping, and by roughly how much.

Three things can happen:

The range ends up entirely on the "better" side. The habit works for you, and the range tells you by about how much. Keep it.

The range ends up entirely on the "worse" side. The habit is hurting. Good to know, and now you can drop it with a clear conscience.

The range hugs "no difference." Whatever effect exists is too small to matter, at least for you. Save your money and move on.

Unlike most studies, you don't have to decide in advance how long to run things. ABMe's maths is built so that you can peek at the result every single day and stop the moment the picture is clear, without fooling yourself. Many of the experiments in the library resolve in five to eight weeks.

Common questions

Why can't I just pick which days to do it?

Because you'd pick badly without meaning to. People tend to start the new habit on a good day, skip it when they're stressed, and try harder on the days they're being measured. None of that is dishonest, it's just human. Handing the choice to a coin flip removes you from the decision, which is the only way to be sure the difference you see is real.

Doesn't switching back and forth make it harder?

Slightly, but that's what makes the answer trustworthy. For some things that linger in your system, the app can build in a gap between switches so one chunk doesn't bleed into the next. The ready-made experiments already have sensible settings for this.

How long does an experiment take?

It depends on how big the effect is and how noisy the measurement is. Big, obvious effects show up in a couple of weeks. Subtle ones take longer. The nice part is that you don't have to guess up front: the range on screen narrows as you go, and you stop when it's clear enough. Most library experiments land in five to eight weeks.

What if I forget, or cave and have the coffee anyway?

It happens. Mark that chunk as "didn't follow it" in the app and carry on. One slip won't ruin the experiment. Pretending you followed it when you didn't is the only thing that will, because then the two piles get mixed up.

Do I need a wearable?

For anything about sleep, heart rate, recovery, or exercise, yes. A wearable is what turns "I think I slept well" into an actual number, and it does the recording for you. Any device that syncs to Apple Health works. For things no device measures, you can type the result in yourself after each chunk.

Is this the same as a medical study?

It uses the same core idea, randomization, that clinical trials and the big tech companies' A/B tests rely on. The difference is the subject. A clinical trial tells you what works on average across thousands of people. ABMe tells you what works for one person: you. Those can be very different answers. ABMe is a tool for curiosity and self-knowledge, not a medical device, and it's no substitute for your doctor.

Who sees my data?

Nobody. ABMe has no accounts and no servers. Everything is stored and calculated on your own phone. If you choose to share a result with the community, you share the result, not your raw health data.

Can I try an experiment someone else ran?

Yes. When someone shares an experiment, you can load their exact setup with one tap and run it on yourself. If the effect shows up for you too, that's much stronger evidence than any single person's story.
